Team Handbook

How we work, what you own, and what "done" means.

For the engineering team · Version 1 · September 2026

You are four people building one website. Two of you could probably build it faster alone — and that is exactly why there are four. The product is the visible output; the thing you are actually here to learn is how to work inside a team that reviews, integrates and ships together.

Everything in this document is a rule we will actually hold you to. Nothing in it is decoration.

1 · Where the project stands

Evolve Academy runs creative-discipline training in Algeria, in person and online, for two audiences: companies (digital marketing and creativity) and high-achieving students (creative skills and internships). Access is sold in packs.

Two pieces of software serve it:

PieceWhat it doesWho builds it
Academy dashboard
evolve_academy_dashboard		 Flutter Web. Staff tooling: admins and teachers manage courses, modules, lessons, video and approvals. It owns the database. Existing team
Website
evolve-web		 Next.js. The public site, and the student platform: courses, lessons, progress, projects, discussion. You
The most important fact in this document

The database is not ours. The schema and its row-level security policies live in the dashboard repository. Our app reads that database as the signed-in user, and holds no service-role key. Postgres decides who may see what; we do not restate those rules in the frontend.

Do not write migrations in our repository. If you need a column, a table or a policy change, raise it — it goes through the dashboard repo. Working around a missing column in the frontend is the one shortcut that gets a pull request closed rather than reviewed.

2 · The phases

  1. Brainstorming and scope. Understand what the academy is actually for. Ends in a one-page product brief — not a wishlist.
  2. Requirements. Split four ways, and each of you reviews someone else's section. Acceptance criteria written as testable statements.
  3. Diagrams and design. A domain model reverse-engineered from the real database, sequence diagrams for the three flows that have real complexity, and lo-fi wireframes.
  4. Implementation. One foundations week in pairs, then two-week sprints with owned areas.

A maintainer coordinates all four, reviews everything before it merges, and is the route to anything that needs the dashboard team.

3 · The foundations week

Before you split into areas, all four of you build the skeleton together, in pairs, rotating daily. Four tracks, two days each.

Three rules that make pairing work

One person stays, one rotates. The person who stays carries the context, so a rotation costs a briefing rather than a restart. Rotating both people means starting over — which is why "we tried pair rotation and it was slow" is usually this mistake.

Driver and navigator, swapping every 25 minutes. Set a timer. The navigator does not touch the keyboard — that is the entire discipline. A navigator who reaches over becomes a second driver, and the pair becomes one person working while another watches.

The handover is written. Whoever rotates off leaves a comment on the draft pull request: what is done, what is half-done, what they would do next. Five minutes, and for most people it is the first time they write for a colleague rather than for a marker.

4 · Your areas, after the foundations week

You each own directories. This is deliberate: four people editing one file is how a sprint ends in merge conflicts instead of a demo.

AreaScope
Design system & public pagesTokens, primitives, home, disciplines, packs, contact. You are a dependency the other three rely on.
Auth, profile & pack gatingSign-in, onboarding, profile, and what each pack unlocks. Security-adjacent: be careful, be reviewed.
Catalogue & playerDisciplines → courses → lesson player → progress. The deepest data work in the project.
CommunityStudent projects, group visibility, comments. You will be blocked on schema that does not exist yet — managing that is part of the job.

A change to someone else's area goes in as a pull request to them. That friction is intentional. Negotiating an interface with a colleague is the skill; silently editing their file is the habit we are training out.

One of you each week is the integrator: you own the health of development, triage anything red, and write the week's release note. Everyone does it at least twice.

5 · The loop

git checkout development
git pull
git checkout -b feat/discipline-cards
# ... work, committing as you go ...
make ci-local                 # everything CI runs, locally, in ~1 min
git push -u origin HEAD
gh pr create                  # bases on development automatically

Branch from development, never from main. Run make ci-local before every push — a red pull request costs four minutes to learn what one minute would have told you.

Open the pull request as a draft when you start, not when you finish. Work nobody can see is work nobody can help with, and "I'll open it when it's ready" is how a 900-line diff arrives on the last day of a sprint.

You cannot break this repository

You cannot push to development or main, force-push them, delete them, or merge a red pull request. That is enforced by organisation rulesets and applies to everyone, including the person who set them up.

This is meant to be freeing rather than restrictive. Push early, push unfinished work, experiment. The worst outcome available to you is a red check.

6 · What the machine checks

Six guards, ordered so the cheapest fails first. Each exists because breaking it caused a real problem; the reason is written at the top of each script in scripts/.

CheckCatches
SecretsA credential in a tracked file. Reports the line, never the value.
Boundarieslib/ importing a component; next/link; process.env outside the config module.
Design tokensA hex colour in markup; pl-/pr- classes that break Arabic.
Component sizeA .tsx file over 300 lines.
Format and lintBiome.
TypecheckEverything else.

These are executable rules, not conventions. If a guard blocks you and the rule is wrong, change the rule in the same pull request and say why. Do not add a quiet exemption — an exemption list nobody reviews is a guard that has stopped working.

7 · Definition of Done

A change is done when all eight are true. Not seven.

  1. Branch named feat/ fix/ or chore/, based on and targeting development
  2. make ci-local green locally, CI green on the pull request
  3. Template filled in — including how it was verified
  4. Approved by one peer, then by a maintainer
  5. Checked on the preview deployment, not only on localhost
  6. UI changes: screenshots at mobile and desktop width, and in Arabic
  7. No new any, no new lint suppressions
  8. Docs updated if behaviour changed

8 · Writing the pull request

The template asks four questions. The third is the one that matters.

"How was this verified" is not for "tests pass" — that is what CI already says. It is for what CI cannot check: which screen you exercised and at what width, whether you loaded the Arabic page and the layout survived, what you checked against real data rather than assumed, and what is still unverified.

The sentence to remember

A declared shortcut is a decision. A discovered one is a defect.

Nobody here has ever been in trouble for writing "I wasn't sure this was the right place for it" in a pull request. People do get pushed back on for hiding it.

Keep pull requests under about 400 changed lines. Over that a reviewer skims instead of reading, and skimmed code is unreviewed code.

9 · Reviewing each other

Every pull request needs one peer review before a maintainer looks at it. Reviewing is not a formality on the way to a merge — it is half of the job you are here to learn. Read the diff, run the branch, comment.

Prefix your comments so the author knows what is blocking:

PrefixMeans
blocking:Must change before merge.
suggestion:Worth considering. The author decides.
nit:Style or taste. Never blocking.
question:You want to understand, not to change.

Without prefixes, everyone treats every comment as fatal and a one-word note stalls a branch for a day.

10 · The sprint rhythm

CeremonyWhenShape
StandupDaily, 15 minWritten three days a week, live two. Writing it is practice at describing work legibly.
PlanningStart of sprintA maintainer facilitates; you estimate, in points relative to one reference story.
ReviewEnd of sprintA live demo on the preview deployment, with the academy manager present.
RetrospectiveEnd of sprint, 30 minExactly one action item, tracked and revisited. Four action items means zero.

Estimates are a planning tool, never a performance measure. The moment they become one you get inflated numbers and silence, and we lose the only signal we had.

11 · The habits that actually cost us

  • Silent blocking. Blocked for 45 minutes means you say so in the channel. Not an hour, not "after lunch". This is the single most expensive habit on a team this size — people routinely burn two days rather than look slow.
  • The big-bang pull request. Draft from day one, under 400 lines, or it does not get reviewed properly.
  • Editing someone else's area quietly. Open a pull request to them and talk about it.
  • Skipping the Arabic check. Every RTL bug this project will have is invisible in French and obvious the moment you load /ar.

12 · Out of scope, and it will stay that way

So that sprint planning is honest about what we are not doing:

  • Payments. You build pack gating — what a pack unlocks. Taking money is a separate problem with a separate provider.
  • Real-time direct messaging. Needs schema that does not exist yet.
  • Video upload from the website. The dashboard does this, and it works.
  • The dashboard itself. Different repository, different language, not yours.
